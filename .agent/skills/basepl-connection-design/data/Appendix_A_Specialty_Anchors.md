However, based on an examination of the details provided in tested base connections, it was found that there are several different strategies, both in terms of the weld termination, the use of runoff bars, and other details related to CJP groove welds, their soundness, and the avoidance of undercut that would influence both fabrication costs and ultimately the local ductility of the base connection. In Section 4.4.2, it is shown that in weak-axis bending, the bearing occurs under the web and tension occurs in the flanges. Because the flanges act as point loads in this configuration, this is fundamentally different from strong-axis bending where bearing is spread over the flange width and can be treated as a uniformly distributed bearing pressure under each flange. In weak-axis bending, the bearing pressure distribution is more difficult to determine, but it is thought that the flange acts as a small footing with the load spread out at an assumed angle from the flange toe. This topic is not treated in detail in this Design Guide because the most common applications for ductile bases occur in moment frames, which typically have strong-axis bending.

5. Design the shear lug.

Referring to the preceding discussion, when anchor rods are used as the yielding element in a weak base, a shear lug is required to transfer the shear force into the footing. The design of the shear lug (i.e., shear lug dimensions, embedment, edge distance, and welds) will depend on the size of the footing. The procedure outlined in Chapter 4, Section 4.3.3, may be used for this purpose. If shear is transferred through the lug, then it is important to check the design for the additional moment due to the shear on the base plate. Assuming the shear lug protrudes 6.00 in. from the bottom of the base plate, and the grout layer is 1.00 in. thick, the resulting lever arm is 2.00 in. This results in an additional moment of 100 kip-in. applied to the base plate, such that the total moment is:

$M_{u,new} = M_u + M_{shear-lug}$

= 3,600 kip-in + (50.0 kips)(1.00 in. + 2.00 in./2)

= 3,700 kip-in.

The anchor diameter is checked against this updated value of moment, resulting in a requirement that $d_{reduced} \ge 0.687$ in. This is still lower than the specified $d_{reduced} = 0.750$ in. Thus, the design does not need to be revised.

## 6.5 SEISMIC DESIGN OF BRACED FRAME BASE PLATE CONNECTIONS

The seismic design of base connections in braced frames has received minimal attention in research. Moreover, a braced frame base connection in seismic situations is typically designed as a strong base connection. Astaneh-Asl (2008) provides some additional guidance regarding such design, although it is not based on research. For the purposes of this Guide, the design procedure outlined in Chapter 4 for exposed base plate connections in braced frames may be used for seismic design as well.
