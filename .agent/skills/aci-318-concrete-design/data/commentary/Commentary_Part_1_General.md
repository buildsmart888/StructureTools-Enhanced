### R1.1—Scope of ACI CODE-318

R1.1.1 The Code includes provisions for the design of concrete used for structural purposes, including plain concrete, concrete containing nonprestressed reinforcement, prestressed reinforcement, or both; and anchoring to concrete. This chapter includes a number of provisions that explain where the Code applies and how it is to be interpreted.

### R1.2—General

R1.2.1 The commentary refers to ACI CODE-318 as "the Code."

R1.2.2 The American Concrete Institute recommends that the Code be adopted in its entirety.

R1.2.3 Committee 318 develops the Code in English, using inch-pound units. Two translations are published by ACI:

(a) In English using SI units (ACI CODE-318-25—Building Code Requirements for Structural Concrete [SI International System of Units])
(b) In Spanish using SI units (ACI CODE-318-25—Requisitos de Reglamento para Concreto Estructural)

R1.2.5 The Code provides minimum requirements and exceeding these minimum requirements is not a violation of the Code.

The licensed design professional may specify project requirements that exceed the minimum requirements of the Code.

## COMMENTARY

In the strength design procedure, the level of safety is provided by a combination of factors applied to the loads and to the nominal strengths of the members in the structure.

The strength of a member or cross section, calculated using standard assumptions and strength equations, along with material properties and dimensions, commonly is referred to as nominal strength and is generally designated $S_n$. Design strength or usable strength of a member or cross section, $\phi S_n$, is obtained by multiplying the nominal strength by a strength reduction factor $\phi$ less than 1.0. This reduction factor is to account for the probability of under-strength due to variation of in-place material strengths and dimensions, uncertainty in the accuracy of the strength design equations, the degree of ductility, potential failure mode of the member, the required reliability, and significance of the failure. These factors are not directly related to the member or the structure.

The Code, or the general building code, prescribes design load combinations $U$ to be resisted in factored load combinations. Each of these load combinations is obtained by multiplying (factoring) by individual load factors and then combined to obtain a factored load $U$. The individual load factors are to account for the variability of the loads, the magnitude of the individual loads, the probability of simultaneous occurrence of various loads, and the consequences of failure under the different load combinations. Required strength is the action effect, load effect, or strength obtained by determining required design strengths.

A typical design approach, where linear-elastic analysis is applied, implies the total factored moments at any individual section are summed algebraically so the designer can determine cases in a factored load combination to determine the design load effects. Where effects of loads are nonlinear—for example, slenderness effects in structural members—U is used simultaneously to determine the nonlinear, factored load effect. The load effects relevant for strength design include factored moments $M_u$, shear forces $V_u$, axial forces $N_u$, and torques $T_u$. Design strengths at critical cross sections are determined for factored loads. The load effects relevant for service design include stresses and deflections.

In the design process and upon completion thereof, a professional should be aware that providing more strength than required does not necessarily lead to a safer structure. Structures generally exhibit complicated behavior, and, for example, increasing longitudinal reinforcement may beyond that required for moment strength as derived from analysis may result in a change in the failure mode from ductile flexure to undesirable brittle shear failure mode. In addition, factors such as P-delta effects or 3-dimensional failure mechanisms may be underlooked for structures expected to behave nonlinearity during earthquakes.
